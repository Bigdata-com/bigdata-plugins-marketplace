# Addendum, 25 and 26 October 2026: audit, fixes and quality evals

## What changed since 23 October

The 23 October build was audited on 25 October against three questions: will the skills be picked up by the hosts, are the numbers and tool steps right, and would a senior portfolio manager or a head of investor relations accept the output. The audit found problems in all three, and every fix was made on a second branch, `skills-audit-fixes-2026-10-25`, so the two builds can be compared. Nothing is merged, pushed or published to any host.

**Would the skills be picked up.** Claude Code gives the skill listing about 1 percent of the context window (about 8,000 characters) and drops descriptions when it overflows; Codex caps the listing at 8,000 characters and shortens descriptions first. The 26 descriptions ran to 12,852 characters, opened with the product name, and carried no wording a corporate reader would use (competitor, peer group, due diligence, investor relations). All 26 were rewritten task-first, at or under 240 characters, with corporate trigger words and explicit boundaries between neighbouring skills (thesis, scenarios and risk; moat and sector; screen and comps). The listing is now 6,758 characters with names. The trigger suite grew from 156 to 182 cases with one corporate-voice prompt per skill: 177 passed on the first run, and the five that fired nothing passed 3 of 3 after their descriptions were retuned with the exact phrasings.

**Were the numbers and tool steps right.** Four library formulas were wrong and were checked against the source texts before fixing: excess cash was divided by the cost of capital and then added again (the steady-state value now adds it once, in the equity bridge); gross capex was labelled net; stock compensation was deducted from net income, which already deducts it; and the ROIC file forbade the comparison that the expectations file depends on (reconciled to a three-year rolling return on incremental capital, a single year never being evidence). Eight skills asked for five fiscal years and received five quarters because the tearsheet defaults to quarters; seven asked for consensus revision history the tool does not hold; two used the credit-factor tools to look for bond prices they do not carry. Those realities are now written into the shared tool guide and every step that depends on them. The credit method was rebuilt on S&P's FFO/debt and debt/EBITDA ranges with a refinancing stress and a guarantor and basket step; the stock-move method scores valuation at the pre-event price; the special-situations arithmetic gained carry, the stock-deal hedge and the annualised spread.

**Would a professional accept the output.** The output standard now names corporate strategy, finance and investor-relations readers, caps tables at eight columns, keeps working marks such as "(verify)" out of deliverables, and closes every report with what would change the reading. Sector, theme, deal-analysis and screen gained a slot for the user's own company; deep-dive takes private companies; country gained a macro method. The library files cited research notes that were never shipped, and the library sync now copies every file a copied file cites, so no reference points at a missing file.

## Quality evals, 26 October

The trigger suite tests which skill fires, not what it produces. On 26 October ten skills were run on live prompts, once with the skill and once without, by fresh Opus agents with the same Bigdata.com tools and no web access. The prompts came from what Bigdata.com showed as live that morning: Meta's negative free cash flow and the AI-debt scare, semiconductors down 18 percent in three weeks, Brent at $115, the ten-year at 5 percent, JetBlue and Cable One credit stress, CoreWeave, data-centre power suppliers, SpaceX reporting as a public company. Five prompts were written in a portfolio manager's voice and five in a corporate voice (head of IR, tier-1 supplier, treasury, head of strategy, corporate venture). Four prompts carried a false premise (Lam "cut guidance", GM "lowered" its 2026 outlook, Deckers "almost 7 percent", the ten-year "back near 5 percent"); both arms corrected all four.

Each report was graded by a Sonnet agent against 11 to 13 fixed assertions: six generic (every fact sourced and dated, no unfinished-work markers, tables at most eight columns, gaps stated, a closing what-to-watch, framed for the stated reader) and five to seven specific to the skill (the valuation's equity bridge adds cash once; the credit note stresses refinancing and places the vendor's claim by layer; the stock-move note anchors valuation at the pre-move price and labels its base rate). The six generic assertions are the output standard's own rules, which the no-skill arm could not know, so the headline gap overstates the difference; the skill-specific assertions and a reading of the credit, stock-move, valuation and deep-dive reports are the better evidence, and they point the same way.

The skill arm scored higher on every pair and cost about 40 percent more tokens and 65 percent more time. What the no-skill reports lost: facts without inline sources or dates (8 of 10), no gaps section (5 of 10), valuation only at the post-drop price, no caveat that consensus history does not exist. What the skill reports got wrong: in 6 of 10 a bracketed citation number pointed at a document whose title could not contain the claim; the Nvidia valuation pushed a raw regression beta of 2.19 through CAPM, reached a 13.9 percent cost of capital, and concluded that even its bull case sat below the price; small text-versus-table slips in most reports (a company called third when its own table says second); every report ran over the skill's page target.

## Tool-agnostic bodies and the second round

The skill bodies named tools and their arguments (`sections`, `interval: annual`, fast mode with a reporting-period filter) because that is where the audit found the broken steps. The better design, and the one the host vendors recommend, is for a skill to say what data a step needs and let the tool descriptions decide the call. On 26 October every body was rewritten that way: "five fiscal years of annual statements", "the transcript for the quarter just reported, with the period fixed rather than inferred", "daily prices for the stock and its index over the window, computed". The shared tool guide carries the map from each data need to a tool, the data realities (five periods per pull, consensus is a snapshot, the corporate calendar holds four event types), and the fallbacks when a tool is not in the account (no computing tool: the standard price windows and dated reports; no batch pull: one company at a time). The facts the skills rely on were listed as requests for the tool descriptions themselves, so that the descriptions can carry them.

Three more library changes came from the evals: citations are attached when the fact is taken and written inline as links, with no numbered list to resolve at the end; length follows the ask, with page counts as guidance; and the valuation method checks the beta against peer and sector figures, keeps the cost of equity inside the range the market applies to comparable companies, and shows the value at the market-implied rate.

Four skills were rerun with the new bodies on the same prompts, graded on the same assertions plus a citation check, with the first-round reports regraded on that check for comparison:

Removing the tool names did not bring back the five-quarters failure: all four runs pulled annual statements when years were wanted, fixed the reporting period for transcripts, and fell back correctly where data was missing. The Nvidia valuation now uses a cost of capital of 11.9 percent, a base case of $197 against a $211 price and the market-implied rate shown. The theme skill's cost fell by a third once the guide named batch pulls for peer sets. Wrong citations fell from eight to five across the four reports; the report that kept three still numbered its citations at the end, so the rule was hardened to "do not number citations" after the round. One regression appeared and was fixed: with the country tearsheet no longer named in the body, the GM review assumed a 4 percent ten-year yield instead of fetching the 5 percent figure, so the tool guide now routes the risk-free rate to the country data.

## Open after 26 October

- The two last library edits (no citation numbers; the risk-free rate from the country data) are untested. One more rerun pass on three or four skills is needed before the branch is called done.
- Text-versus-table slips remain in three of four second-round reports. A check item in each skill's "Checks before sending" is the next thing to try; the library rule alone did not remove them.
- The tool-description requests (17 items: annual default and five periods, no consensus history, response sizes, the market tearsheet's change columns, what the computing tool holds, credit-factor limits) need to go to the MCP team.
- Four skill bodies are over 3,000 words after the pass (credit, deal-analysis, earnings-review, special-situations).
- The quality evals ran once per arm, so each score carries a point or two of noise.
