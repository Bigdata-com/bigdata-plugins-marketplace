# Earnings review

## The job

The note the morning after a print: what the company reported against what was expected, what changed in the outlook, whether the quarter changed the long-term picture or only the quarter, and what it means for the person asking. Size it to the question: "did Nike beat" is a results table and the guidance line; "analyse the quarter" is the full note.

## Who is asking

A portfolio manager with a thesis wants it tested: which premises the quarter confirmed, weakened or broke, and whether the stock's move matches the change in long-term cash flow. A corporate reader (a supplier, a customer, a competitor, or the company's own investor-relations team) wants what the print means for their business: demand, pricing, cost plans, programme timing, how the Street read it. For them the valuation refresh, the beta-adjusted reaction and the thesis vocabulary are noise; the guidance comparison, the changes in management's language and the read-across are the substance. Decide which reader before gathering, and let their questions set the order of the note.

## What a complete answer covers

A checklist, not an outline. Cover what the question needs.

- Results against consensus and guidance, with the surprise placed against the company's own record.
- What made the beat or miss, only as far as the company discloses it, each part labelled recurring or one-time, with cash against earnings.
- Guidance, prior against new, with dates, and what management stopped saying: metrics dropped, claims softened, the tone of the Q&A.
- Whether the long-term outlook changed: consensus before and after, implied growth before and after, and the classification (a change in the long-term outlook, a short-term event, or unclear).
- The thesis check, for a reader with a thesis, or how the Street received it for everyone else: revisions, targets, ratings and the reasons given, dated.
- Quality signals for the quarter, each with the threshold that would turn noise into a flag next quarter.
- The market reaction against the index and, for a move of 10% or more, against the base rates.
- The peer read-across: what the print says for competitors, suppliers and customers, and what they said.
- The next date and what it resolves.

## Where this goes wrong

- **The prior guidance taken from memory or the press.** It comes from the previous quarter's call, quoted with its date. Annual guidance may have been set earlier in the year and updated since.
- **A premise in the question taken as fact.** "GM lowered its 2026 outlook" was a narrowed range with the midpoint unchanged. Check the premise against the release before building on it, and say so plainly if it does not hold.
- **Consensus before the print invented.** The estimates feed has no history. The before column comes from research and news dated before the release, labelled as such, or is marked not found.
- **The beat decomposed further than the company discloses.** Where there is no bridge, say so; never estimate a split.
- **One quarter breaking a multi-year thesis**, or a guidance cut on the linchpin driver called noise.
- **A 3% move run through the 10% base-rate tables.** Below 10% against the index the published samples do not apply; stop at the decomposition.
- **The reader's question answered last.** The GM note for a supplier put demand, cost plans and programme timing after eleven investor sections. They go first.

## Data

From Bigdata.com: profile and quote; the latest quarter against consensus and the next report date; consensus with its vintage, ratings and targets; quarterly statements and segments for the quarter and the year-ago quarter; five fiscal years of annual statements (annual interval) for the rolling ROIIC; the release and the full transcript for the quarter reported, and the full transcript of the previous quarter's call; the 10-Q if it is out; daily prices for the stock and its index over the event day and the 60 months before it. Searches, one aspect each: the Street's revisions and reasons since the release; what competitors, suppliers and customers said; consensus quoted before the release. The sector primer for the metrics that matter and the accounting traps.

`working-with-bigdata.md` maps each of these to a tool and says what to do when a tool is missing. Read it before the first call.

## Write

Follow the house style. Open with the answer in the reader's terms: what happened against expectations, what changed, what it means for them, the next date. Then the sections the question needs, in the order the question asked them; the results and guidance tables come early because everything else rests on them. A row exists only where there is a figure. A reaction note is the results table, the guidance table and the thesis or Street line, two or three sentences each.

## Before sending

1. The premise of the question was checked against the release.
2. Prior guidance is quoted from the earlier call with its date.
3. Every figure in the opening agrees with its table, and consensus carries its vintage.
4. The beat is split only as far as the company disclosed it.
5. Nothing in the note serves only an investor when the reader is not one; no rating, no target.
