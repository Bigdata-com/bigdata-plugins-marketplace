# Consumer and retail

Retailers (broadline, specialty, grocery, off-price, e-commerce), restaurants, branded consumer goods (apparel, durables, food, beverages, household and personal products) and consumer services. The sector spans two GICS sectors with different behaviour: consumer discretionary sales track the economy closely, consumer staples barely do. Say which one a company belongs to before using any base rate.

## How the sector makes money

- Retailers earn a thin margin on fast-turning inventory and a large, mostly leased store or fulfilment base. Brands earn a wide gross margin and spend heavily on advertising, promotion and distribution.
- ROIC = NOPAT / sales x sales / invested capital. A low-cost retailer can earn 20 percent at a 4 percent margin with 5x capital turnover; a luxury seller earns the same at 20 percent and 1x. Cost leadership shows up in turnover, differentiation in margin. Know which one the company claims before judging a margin change.
- Revenue = revenue per store x stores. Comparable-store growth needs little capital and lifts ROIC; new-store growth needs capex and creates value only if the new stores earn above the cost of capital.
- For a low-ROIC retailer, one point of ROIC was worth about 15 percent of value against about 5 percent for one point of growth; for a high-ROIC consumer-goods company the order reverses (a stylised comparison). Returns matter more than growth for most retailers.
- Value drivers: traffic, ticket and mix; gross margin net of markdowns and shrink; occupancy and labour leverage; inventory turnover; store or unit economics; brand pricing power.

## Metrics that matter

| Metric | Definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Comparable sales | Sales growth at stores or channels open a full year, under the company's own definition | Isolates organic demand. Check the definition (e-commerce included or not, closure treatment, 53rd week). |
| Traffic and ticket | Transactions; average transaction value | Ticket-driven comps with falling traffic often mask share loss; traffic-driven comps are stronger. |
| Price, volume and mix (brands) | Organic growth split | Price-led growth with volume decline tests elasticity. The average price elasticity across about 370 goods was about 1.76; staples are usually less elastic, discretionary categories more. |
| Gross margin and markdowns | Gross profit / sales; clearance activity | Inventory growing faster than sales for two or more quarters has often preceded markdowns. |
| Inventory turnover, GMROI | COGS / average inventory; gross margin / average inventory at cost | Turnover ranges from more than 10x in grocery to under 3x in furniture and luxury; compare within format only. |
| Sales per square foot, unit volumes | Sales / selling area; average unit volume for restaurants | Productivity of the fixed base; falling productivity alongside unit growth suggests over-expansion. |
| New-unit economics | Cash-on-cash return or payback per new store | Tests whether unit growth creates value; new units often open below mature productivity and cannibalise. |
| Channel economics | Margin by store, e-commerce, wholesale, direct-to-consumer | Direct-to-consumer gross margin is higher than wholesale, but fulfilment, returns and paid acquisition can leave operating margin lower. |

Most are not tearsheet fields: take them from filings, transcripts and investor-day material through search.

## Financial statements and accounting traps

- Leases. Under IFRS 16 and ASC 842 most store leases sit on the balance sheet, but EBITDA under IFRS excludes rent while US GAAP operating-lease cost stays in operating expense. Make EV and the earnings measure consistent (both include lease liabilities and exclude rent, or neither). The "8 times rent" shortcut works only near a 6 percent cost of debt and a 15-year life.
- Coverage: use EBITDAR / (interest + rent) for retailers.
- Vendor allowances, supplier-finance programs and extended payables flatter working capital and gross margin; check the payables days trend.
- Gift cards and loyalty programs create deferred revenue; breakage income can be a material and adjustable source of profit.
- Inventory method: LIFO reserves, retail-method write-downs and reserve releases move margin; compare peers on the same basis.
- Calendar: 53-week years, shifted holidays and fiscal year-ends in late January distort year-on-year comparisons.
- Franchise models: system sales are not revenue; refranchising lifts margin and ROIC while shrinking revenue.

## Profit pool and competitive dynamics

- Household products (four-firm revenue concentration about 65.5 percent), personal products (56.3) and tobacco (71.8) were concentrated; textiles and apparel (18.9), hotels, restaurants and leisure (15.5), food products (20.0) and auto components (18.0) were fragmented; internet and catalog retail was near the 40 percent line at 38.5, with the top-10 share of invested capital rising from 58 to 72 percent over 2005 to 2015 (listed companies, data to June 2016).
- Concentration measured by HHI: breakfast cereal about 2,425, snack food 1,984, tires 1,540, apparel 44 (2007 US Census data). Readings above 1,800 suggest reduced rivalry.
- Department stores had a low median ROIC (about 9 percent, US, 1965 to 2007) and persistently low growth; household and personal products and beverages had persistently high ROIC.
- Mix can outweigh volume. Split every margin change into price, mix and volume.
- Scale economies are distinct from operating leverage: scale lowers unit cost as the firm grows, while operating leverage spreads fixed cost over more units.
- Retailers whose business rests on image can be damaged irreversibly in distress; weigh balance-sheet risk more heavily here than in capital-intensive sectors.

Read: staples fade slowest of all sectors (78 percent of the excess CFROI spread has remained after four years), discretionary next. Sector means have small dispersion, so fade toward the sector mean rather than a mid-cycle estimate. Discretionary sales are the most GDP-sensitive of any sector; for housing-linked, durable and big-ticket categories, normalise sales and margin to a mid-cycle level before applying a multiple.

## Valuation conventions and pitfalls

- P/E for staples and mature brands with comparable capital structures; EV/EBIT or lease-consistent EV/EBITDA (or EV/EBITDAR with leases in EV) across retailers with different lease intensity.
- Pair every multiple with ROIC and growth; a cheap-looking retailer often has low ROIC after leases. State how peers were chosen and show the multiple with and without high-multiple outliers.
- Price unit growth through store-level returns, not through a growth premium.
- Owned real estate is a separate asset: value it outside the operating multiple and remove the rent it would carry.
- Do not apply e-commerce or direct-to-consumer multiples without segment-level profitability.

## Questions for the earnings call

1. How did comparable sales split between traffic and ticket, and between price and mix?
2. How much of gross margin change came from markdowns, freight, shrink and mix?
3. Is inventory per store growing faster than sales, and how much is aged?
4. What are payback and productivity of the last two years' new stores against the mature base?
5. What is the operating margin of the e-commerce or direct-to-consumer channel after fulfilment and returns?
6. How did price elasticity respond to the last price increase, and how is private label share moving?
7. How much of the year's margin depends on supplier terms or vendor funding?

## What to watch at the macro level

Real disposable income and wage growth, consumer credit and delinquency, savings rate, consumer confidence, housing turnover (for durables and home), gasoline prices, tariffs and freight costs for importers, input commodities (grains, cotton, packaging), and currency. Staples respond more to input costs and pricing than to GDP; discretionary responds to GDP and credit.
