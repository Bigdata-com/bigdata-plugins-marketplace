# Materials

Chemicals (commodity and specialty), metals and mining (steel, aluminium, copper, iron ore, precious metals), construction materials (cement, aggregates), containers and packaging, and paper and forest products. Commodity producers are price takers whose returns follow industry capacity; specialty and local-market businesses (specialty chemicals, aggregates, some packaging) behave more like industrials. Classify the company on that axis first.

## How the sector makes money

- Commodity producers: margin = market price minus unit cash cost, times volume. Position on the industry cost curve decides who earns a return at mid-cycle prices; the lowest-cost quartile earns through the cycle, the highest-cost quartile only near the peak.
- Specialty producers: margin from formulation, service and switching costs; price is set against customer value, not raw-material cost, and passes through input changes with a lag.
- Local-market businesses: cement and aggregates are heavy and costly to transport, so competition is regional; a quarry or plant near a growing market can hold price.
- Value drivers: the price deck, cost-curve position, volume and utilisation, input costs (energy, feedstock, ore), reserve life and grade (mining), capital discipline and the timing of capacity additions.
- Median real sales growth of materials companies correlated about 0.53 with US real GDP growth (top 1,000 global, 1950 to 2014). Industrial production and China construction usually explain more than headline GDP.

## Metrics that matter

| Metric | Definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Realised price against benchmark | Price received / index price | Grade, quality and contract lags move margin. |
| Unit cash cost and cost-curve position | Cash cost per tonne or unit; quartile on the industry curve | Determines survival at trough prices. |
| Spread | Product price minus feedstock cost (for example, ethylene against ethane or naphtha) | Commodity chemical earnings are a spread business. |
| Utilisation | Output / nameplate capacity, company and industry | The volume-to-margin link (below). |
| Volume, price, mix split | Organic change decomposed | Price-led margin gains reverse with price. |
| Reserve life, grade, strip ratio (mining) | Reserves / annual output; metal content; waste moved per tonne | Grade decline raises unit cost even at constant output. |
| Sustaining against growth capex | Capex to hold capacity / capex to add it | Separates free cash flow from reinvestment. |
| Energy intensity | Energy cost / total cost | Aluminium, cement, fertiliser and chlor-alkali are exposed to power and gas prices. |

Most are not tearsheet fields: take them from filings, reserve statements and transcripts through search.

## Operating leverage and capacity utilisation

- Materials had the highest operating margin beta of any sector: 0.193 at one year and 0.155 at three, against 0.11 for all sectors Analyst forecast errors are larger where beta is high, and larger for metals than for food. Widen ranges accordingly.
- Changes in capacity utilisation correlated about 0.6 with changes in operating margin (US, 1967 to 2015). Ask for utilisation and industry operating rates on every call.
- Forecast downside and upside with different incremental margins.
- Supply drives the cycle: commodity chemical producers invest when ROIC is high, capacity arrives in large increments, and utilisation and ROIC fall about two years later. Capital timing matters more than operations in this sector.
- S-curve warning: capacity built on mid-curve demand growth overshoots (colour TV capacity 14 million units against peak sales of 6 million). Test announced capacity against a demand peak.

## Mid-cycle normalisation

Normalise margins and returns to mid-cycle before any multiple or terminal value. Mid-cycle is a point on the long-run trend line, not a peak or a trough.

1. Set a mid-cycle price deck from the cost curve (the cash cost of the marginal producer plus a return on sustaining capital), the long-dated futures where they exist, and a long-run real average; state it.
2. Rebuild margin and ROIC at that deck and at mid-cycle utilisation, after structural changes (closures, mix toward specialty).
3. Cross-check against the sector mean below and the company's own returns over decades; a ten-year average was a better guide than any single year.
4. Weight a normal-cycle case against a new-trend case, each with supply and demand reasoning, with probabilities that sum to 100.

The reason: consensus EPS for cyclicals has historically trended upward through peaks and troughs (36 US cyclicals, 1985 to 1997, including primary metals), so a peak-earnings P/E looks cheap.

## Financial statements and accounting traps

- Inventory holding gains and LIFO against FIFO: in rising price markets, inventory profits inflate earnings and are low quality. Compare peers on the same inventory basis.
- Impairments reset the asset base and lower future depreciation; write-downs used to cut depreciation flatter later returns. Report ROIC before and after.
- Depreciation and depletion adequacy: compare depreciation to the property base and to sales across peers; check depletion life against reserve life.
- Pensions: remove expected return on plan assets from operating profit.
- Environmental, reclamation and asset retirement obligations are debt-like.
- Provisional pricing in copper and other concentrates marks revenue to later prices; separate it from operating performance.
- Equity-accounted joint ventures are common in mining and chemicals; value them outside the operating multiple.

## Profit pool and competitive dynamics

- Metals and mining (1,481 listed firms, four-firm revenue concentration about 8.2 percent) and chemicals (1,048 firms, 14.3 percent) were among the most fragmented industries; the top-10 share of chemicals invested capital was about 23 percent (listed companies, data to June 2016).
- Commodities had a median ROIC of about 5.8 percent and paper and forest products a persistently low one; chemicals and metals and mining were classed as cyclical (US, 1965 to 2007).
- Industry does not define destiny: low-cost producers earn a positive spread through the cycle while others destroy value. Build the profit pool for the subindustry over three to five years at least.
- Resource uniqueness is a real advantage (Alcoa's bauxite) and so is local scale (aggregates); a moat claim for a commodity producer needs one of these.
- Low-end disruption: mini-mills displaced integrated steel mills from the bottom of the market.
- Replacement cost limits returns: when market values implied aluminium fabricating capacity above its replacement cost of about $1,000 per tonne a year, overbuilding followed.

## Valuation conventions and pitfalls

- EV/EBITDA on mid-cycle EBITDA within peers of similar capital intensity; EV/EBIT where depreciation burdens differ.
- Mining: net asset value from mine-by-mine DCF at a stated price deck, with price sensitivity. Undeveloped deposits carry option value. Use option methods only where value and volatility of the underlying are reliable.
- EV per unit of capacity against replacement cost: fair value per unit = sustainable NOPLAT per unit x (1 - g / ROIC) / (WACC - g). The multiple alone says nothing about profitability.
- For commodity producers without a cost or resource advantage, a terminal spread near zero is the default; steady-state P/E = 1 / cost of equity.
- P/B is useful only next to ROE, and book value after impairments is not replacement cost.
- Load `references/valuation.md` and `references/multiples-and-peers.md`.

## Questions for the earnings call

1. What were utilisation and industry operating rates, and what capacity is coming on across the industry in the next two years?
2. How did margin change split between price, volume, mix, input costs and efficiency?
3. Where does the company sit on the cost curve, and what moved unit cost?
4. What is sustaining capex, and what returns are expected on growth projects at a mid-cycle deck?
5. How much of the price change passes through to customers, and with what lag?
6. (Mining) How are grade, strip ratio and reserve life trending?
7. What would the company do with capital at the trough: buy assets, buy back stock, or cut?

## What to watch at the macro level

Industrial production and construction activity, especially China property and infrastructure; global steel, aluminium and chemical capacity additions and closures; energy and feedstock prices; freight; the US dollar; trade policy and tariffs on metals; and environmental regulation that raises cost or closes capacity. Treat any current price as a scenario input, never as a forecast.
