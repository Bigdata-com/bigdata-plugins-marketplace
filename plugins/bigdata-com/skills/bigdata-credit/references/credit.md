# Credit analysis

Can the company meet its obligations through a downturn and a refinancing, what does each layer of the capital structure recover if it cannot, and do the equity and debt prices tell the same story? Credit risk is leverage times the instability of cash flow, so judge the business before any ratio. A bond's gain is capped at its yield and its loss can be total, so look for what can go wrong. Give the balance of evidence, not a rating or a call.

## Inputs

| Input | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| Debt, notes payable, leases, cash, current assets and liabilities, inventory, receivables, goodwill, PP&E, equity; EBIT, EBITDA, interest, CFO, capex, depreciation, FCF, dividends, buybacks | Company statements, five fiscal years |
| Net debt, EV, market cap, Altman Z and Piotroski scores | Company key metrics and financial ratios |
| Forward EBIT and EBITDA | Estimates, to four years out |
| Rent, maturity schedule, credit agreement and covenants, rating actions, pension deficit, contingent liabilities, deconsolidated entities, related-party loans | Filings and news, then the full document |
| Credit news signals | The credit-factor tool: strongest positive and negative credit-news catalysts by day, week or month. No prices, yields or ratios |
| Bond prices, yields, spreads, agency commentary, fixed-floating split, hedges | Not in the data. Quoted figures from news, research and the debt note; otherwise state the gap |
| Cost of debt, stress shock, scenario enterprise values | Not in the data. State each once, with its basis |

## The claim and its obligor

Name the security and its rank, and the legal entity that owes it. Holding-company debt can default while the operating subsidiaries carry on, because debt at the subsidiaries ranks ahead of it on their assets. From the debt and guarantor notes, record which subsidiaries guarantee the claim, the share of EBITDA and assets outside the guarantor group, the collateral, and any covenant baskets that let assets or collateral move away from existing lenders (drop-down and non-pro-rata uptier transactions).

## Stability of the business

Show the five-year EBITDA range, its worst year, the sector's EBITDA volatility and what drives the variability. A stable record is not proof of a stable business: a carmaker's preferred covered 26 times proved less safe than a grocery chain's covered 6 times, because steady demand, many stores and fast inventory turns made the grocer's earnings dependable.

## Coverage and leverage ratios

Compute every ratio on the same period, with total interest, never cash interest alone.

- **Coverage** = EBIT / total interest. Variants: EBITA or EBITDA / interest, and EBITDAR / (interest + rent) for lessees such as retailers. EBITDA over cash interest overstates safety.
- **Net debt / EBITDA**, with net debt = debt + capitalised leases - excess cash, and excess cash = cash above about 2 percent of revenue or the industry's own minimum. Prefer it when low-coupon convertibles or cheap short-term debt will reprice, because coverage falls at refinancing.
- **FCF / total debt.**
- **Market-adjusted debt ratio** = market value of common stock / funded debt, a leading margin-of-safety test. Book ratios such as debt / book equity are the least useful.
- **Leverage and ROE**: ROE = ROIC + (D / E) x [ROIC - (1 - T) x kd]. Debt raises ROE and its sensitivity to ROIC.
- **Free cash flow** = after-tax earnings + depreciation and amortisation - capex, or CFO - capex - stock compensation.
- **Current ratio** = current assets / current liabilities; **quick ratio** = (current assets - inventory) / current liabilities.
- **FFO** = EBITDA - cash interest - cash taxes. **Adjusted debt** = reported debt + lease liabilities + unfunded pensions - surplus cash.

## Coverage stress and refinancing stress

- **Stressed coverage** = EBIT x (1 - s) / interest. Make the shock s at least as large as the worst year in the window or the sector's EBITDA volatility; a 20 percent cut is the usual illustration.
- **Refinancing stress**: re-price every maturity within 24 months, and the floating-rate share of the rest, at the current yield for the rating, quoted from news or research or stated as an assumption. Recompute interest, coverage and FCF / debt, alone and combined with the EBIT shock. State the fixed-floating split and the hedges.

## Liquidity and the maturity wall

List debt due within 24 months with amounts and dates, short-term bank loans, cash, undrawn facilities, and covenant headroom. Rising short-term bank debt almost always precedes financial difficulty, and large near maturities are a frequent cause of insolvency.

## Claims outside the debt line and cash flow

Count leases, pensions after tax, contingent liabilities, guarantees, deconsolidated entities, securitised receivables and related-party loans. Check for hidden debt: goodwill as a share of equity, PP&E growing faster than revenue, a shift from short- to long-term debt, deconsolidations. Compute free cash flow over five periods against depreciation: EBITDA is not cash flow, and capex below depreciation for long is gradual liquidation.

## Recovery by layer

For a stressed company, separate the ongoing business from assets available for distribution, list claims at face by seniority, and value the business at two or more stated enterprise values.

- Recovery of layer i in scenario s: R_i = min(1, max(0, (EV_s - claims senior to i) / claim_i)).
- Equity_s = max(EV_s - total claims, 0), weighted across scenarios by probability.
- Use the face value of debt, never the market value of distressed debt, and state scenario enterprise values rather than historical recovery rates.
- Classify each claim as senior (covered), fulcrum (partly covered, most sensitive to value) or junior (option-like).

## Comparing the layers

Enterprise value implied by layer i at price q_i (fraction of face), with everything junior worth zero: EV_i = claims senior to i + q_i x claim_i. If the equity has market value while EV_i is below total claims, the two markets disagree; one security can be dear while another is cheap.

## Historical thresholds

Use the logic, not the level.

| Test | Figure | Population and period |
|---|---|---|
| Current and quick ratio | 2 to 1 and 1 to 1, with industry exceptions | US industrial companies, 1930s |
| Coverage | Earnings about 3 times interest on average over seven years, a floor not to fall far below | US industrial bonds, 1930s |
| Unseasoned industrial bonds and preferreds | Speculative unless priced below 70 | US issues, 1930s |

Want a rising profit trend, a good current year and a margin over interest in every year; if one fails, require coverage well above the minimum.

## Distress signals

- Short-term bank loans and notes payable rising; large maturities within 24 months.
- Coverage below 1.0; debt above tangible assets.
- Inventories and receivables building while cash falls.
- Dividends held while free cash flow is weak; stressed companies are almost always late to cut.
- PIK, zero-coupon or interest-reset features, which postpone default and raise ultimate risk; a new yield measure such as yield to reset replacing the standard one.
- An equity collapse raising market leverage while cash flow is thin.
- Related-party and off-balance-sheet loans.
- Interim reporting dropped as results weaken.
- Negative operating cash flow before interest in a fast-deteriorating business: avoid its fixed income.

## Reading the evidence

- Altman Z and Piotroski scores are flags as the data set computes them; apply no cutoffs of your own.
- A bond well below par offers more upside and less downside than one near par of the same credit. A high coupon does not pay for substantial risk to principal.
- Default rates look low when issuance grows fast, interest was prefunded, or exchange offers impair holders without a default; group defaults by year of issue.
- Size is no defence when debt is disproportionately large. Coverage that rests on a low coupon is not safety.
- Review a credit view at least quarterly.

## Where the method changes

- Banks and insurers: interest is operating and there is no net debt; use capital ratios.
- Cyclicals: use mid-cycle EBITDA and the sector's EBITDA volatility.
- Loss-making companies: coverage on a deficit is meaningless. Set cash and undrawn facilities against the cash burn and the maturities.
- High inflation: use EBITDA / interest.
- REITs, project finance and securitisations: these ratios and scales do not apply as written; say so.
