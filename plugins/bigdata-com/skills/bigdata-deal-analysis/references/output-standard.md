# Output standard

The rules every Bigdata.com report follows, kept in one file so the skills do not repeat them. The reader is someone who will act on the report. They want a high-quality, actionable write-up about the company and the question they asked. Everything that went into producing it stays out of it: the note reads as the work of an analyst who knows the method, not as a record of the method.

**Answer first.** Open with what the evidence shows, what the price implies where that applies, what it means, and what would change the reading: four to eight sentences, or four to six short bullets, under 200 words, no labels or run-in headings. Then the body in the order of the question. End on what would change the reading, or on what to watch.

**Structure follows the question.** The parts the user asked for come first, in the user's order, each answered in full before anything the skill adds; a question with four parts gets four answers a reader can find by their names, and the last part the user asked (where we sit, what it means for us, what to expect) is answered as fully as the first, with the same arithmetic, ending in the figures or terms the question asked for; a list of observations about it is not an answer. A skill says what a complete answer covers; it is not a section list, and a method the skill describes applies only where the question reaches it. The exhibits a complete note of its kind carries (each skill names its own) are on the page as tables whenever the question reaches them, because the reader looks for them by name. A section with nothing behind it is not written. A table appears only where the reader needs the comparison, has a row only where there is a figure, and an empty cell stays blank rather than saying "not found" or "unclear". Sub-headings state the finding, not the step that produced it. If the user gave a structure, length or format, follow it.

**Tables.** A table is read on a phone without scrolling, so it has at most four columns, or five when every cell is a short number or when the domain's own form needs the fifth (a capital structure by rank, results against consensus and guidance), and a heading of one to three words ("P/E 2027", "Return on capital", "What it moves"). Unit, period, basis and any assumption go in one italic line under the table, not in the headings. One measure per column: never "6.8x / 7.7x / 15.7x" in a cell; a third period is a second table or a sentence. A comparison that needs more measures is split by measure, or the extra measures go into prose. Cells may hold a short sentence when the table compares cases or scenarios (what happens, what triggers it, who it hits); that kind of table reads better than a numeric grid for a reader deciding what to do. The reference tables in the working notes are dense by design and are never a layout for the page.

**Numbers.** Unit, currency and period on every figure, with the table-level ones in the line under the table; consensus with its date. An assumption that a conclusion rests on is stated once, in plain words, in the sentence or table note where it matters ("assuming retail at 25 times trailing profit and an 11 percent cost of capital"), never under a label such as "Assumptions:", "Method:" or "How the weights were set:". Nothing else is labelled: no "derived", "assumed", "judgment", "reference class" or similar words, no note on how a figure was computed. Ranges for forecasts and values. Before sending, read every number in the opening against the table it came from.

**One figure per quantity.** A quantity has one value in the note, and the title, the opening, the headings and the tables repeat it unchanged; where two measures of the same thing exist (gross and net of tax, reported and adjusted), the note picks one, says which, and uses it throughout. A ratio, return or implied figure the note relies on shows its inputs once, in the sentence or the table note where it first appears, so the reader can rebuild it. The cases in any scenario, sensitivity or recovery table partition the outcomes: one state of the company per case, one cut-off per metric, and no case that both performs and restructures.

**Citations.** A fact taken from a document carries a link with the source name and date right after the claim, attached as the fact is captured. Prefer the primary document. Never number citations. Figures from Bigdata.com data sets with no document behind them are covered by one Data line at the end naming the data sets and the retrieval date. Methods, formulas and historical statistics are the writer's own knowledge: they are stated, never cited, and the skill's files, formulas, tables and primers are never named on the page. A historical statistic carries its population and period in the sentence where it first appears ("among companies with more than $50 billion of sales, 1950 to 2015"), because without them the reader cannot weigh it; later figures from the same population say "the same history" rather than repeat it.

**Gaps.** When something material could not be found, say so in one line where it would have gone, or in a short Gaps line at the end, in plain words about the fact ("no consensus for AWS margin was published"), not about the tools or the feed. Documents often carry a figure the structured data lacks; search before stating a gap. Never fill one from memory or the open web without saying so.

**No recommendation on a security.** The report describes the balance of evidence and what would change it. No buy, hold, sell, rating, stance, position size or price target of its own. "Is it a buy" gets what the price assumes and what the evidence says about that. A choice the user asked about in their own business (what to budget, which terms to ask for, which option) is answered with its numbers; that is not a recommendation. A reader's own decision (buy back or pay a dividend, which deal structure, whether a target is worth a closer look) gets the arithmetic of each option side by side, and when the decision turns on a threshold, the opening states it on the note's own numbers: the price up to which a deal earns its cost of capital, the synergy a premium has to earn back, the outcome that would change the answer.

**Language.** Plain sentences, active verbs, the company and the ticker by name. A code name, project name or contract term is explained where it first appears. No hype words, no exclamation marks, no emojis. Facts, then what they mean, then what would change the reading. The note never describes its own making: how a figure was chosen, what was searched, what the writer treats as a judgment.

**Length.** As long as the actionable content and no longer.

**Footer.** The two blocks below, verbatim, after the Data line in every user-facing deliverable, including a one-line answer. The report ends on the disclaimer. Nothing after it offers to save, monitor or build anything.
