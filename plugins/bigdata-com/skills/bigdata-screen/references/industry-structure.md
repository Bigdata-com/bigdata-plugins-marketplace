# Industry structure and moat

Whether a company's return above its cost of capital will last. Test the industry first and the company's advantage second. A moat has a magnitude (the spread over the cost of capital times the capital earning it) and a duration. An advantage passes two tests over a full cycle: a return above the cost of capital and above competitors. Conclude with the balance of evidence, an assumed duration in years and what would change it, never a rating. The spread and the margin and turnover split come from roic.md.

## Inputs

| Input | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| ROIC, margins, revenue and invested capital for the company and each peer | Company tearsheet: statements, ratios and key metrics |
| Revenue by product and geography, five years | Revenue segmentation |
| Peer candidates | A company screen, peers named in filings, the tearsheet's M&A section |
| Industry size and shares, supplier and customer concentration, contract terms, prices, capacity, entrants, exits, retention, patents | By search, one aspect per call: filings (business, risk factors) and transcripts for suppliers, customers and contracts; research for shares; news and transcripts for prices, capacity, entrants and price wars |
| Hiring by rivals and entrants | Hiring trends, news |
| Cost of capital | Not in the data. Build it as valuation.md (cost of capital) sets out |

## Industry map

Analyse the business unit, not the conglomerate. Give its life-cycle phase and why: innovation (rising returns, heavy reinvestment), fading (excess but declining), mature (near the cost of capital) or subpar. Class the industry: fragmented, emerging, mature, declining, international, network or hypercompetitive.

Map suppliers, customers, complementors, regulators and potential entrants, the firms in order of dominance, and the payment terms and incentives between them. Compare the value chain (R&D, supply chain, operations, marketing and sales, service) with peers'. Value created = willingness to pay - opportunity cost. A chain like the peers' means competition to be the best, and price wars.

## Profit pool

- Width = each firm's share of industry invested capital. Height = ROIC - WACC. Area = economic profit = invested capital x (ROIC - WACC).
- Use three- to five-year averages, today against five years ago. The statements give five annual periods; a longer comparison needs filings.
- Ask: is the pool positive on average, who holds the width, are high-spread firms gaining width or only spread, which wide firms destroy value.
- The best industries contain value destroyers and the worst contain creators. Show the dispersion, not only the average. Among firms sustaining high profit, industry explained 44 percent of the variance, the segment itself 34 and the corporate parent 19; among low performers the segment explained 90 percent or more (US businesses, 1981 to 1994, about 58,000 firm-years).

## Concentration and HHI

- HHI = 10,000 x the sum of squared market shares (as fractions) of the 50 largest firms. Shares of 40, 30, 20 and 10 percent give 3,000. Above 1,800 suggests reduced rivalry.
- Four-firm ratio = revenue share of the four largest firms. Below 40 percent is competitive. Pair it with the top-ten share of invested capital.
- Four-firm ratios, global listed companies, 2015: tobacco 71.8%, household products 65.5%, automobiles 46.4%, software 39.3%, banks 15.6%, chemicals 14.3%, metals and mining 8.2%.

Rivalry also rises with similar goals and ownership, asset specificity that keeps firms in, variable demand with high fixed costs (capacity added at the peak, price wars in the trough) and slow growth, which makes share gains zero-sum.

## Share stability

Share stability = (1 / N) x the sum over competitors of |share(t) - share(t-5)|, in percentage points, where N is the number of competitors. Two points or less over five years is stable. Across consumer and technology industries the measure ran from 2 to 9 points (2007 to 2012).

## Pricing power

Take the five-year price trend and split each margin change into price, mix and volume (operating-leverage-and-tam.md, sources of margin change). Stable or rising prices go with a slow-cycle industry, falling prices with a fast-cycle one; across US product categories from 2006 to 2011 the extremes ran from about +10 to -9 percent a year. Ask whether the industry passed supplier cost increases on. A 1 percent price change has moved demand by 1.76 percent on average (about 370 goods).

## Entry and exit

Test entry first: where barriers are low, entry pushes returns toward the cost of capital whatever the other forces show. Barriers: asset specificity; minimum efficient scale against market size; excess capacity; incumbent reputation; precommitment contracts; licences and patents (time-limited); the learning curve; network effects; exit barriers.

Learning curve: unit cost falls by a fixed share each time cumulative output doubles, about 20 percent for the median product. Project cost from cumulative production, not from time, and set a floor at input costs.

| Base rate | Figure | Population and period |
|---|---|---|
| Entry and exit | About 11 of every 100 firms enter and 10 leave each year | US firms, 1977 to 2010 |
| Survival | 75% of establishments reach one year, 45 to 50% reach five; under half of public companies reach ten years | US establishments and listed companies |
| Entrants | About one third of incumbent size; about 80% exit within ten years | US entrants |
| Listed-company life | Half-life about 10 years; implied S&P 500 tenure 24 years | US listed companies, 1975 to 2009; S&P 500, 1958 to 2016 |

Incumbents most often deter new products with advertising (62 percent report frequent use) and R&D and patents (56); limit pricing is rare (8).

## Bargaining power

Test root causes, not an attractiveness label.

- Suppliers are strong when more concentrated than the industry, without substitutes, costly to switch from, selling the industry a small share of their output, supplying a critical input, or able to integrate forward.
- Buyers are strong when concentrated, when the product is a large share of their cost or undifferentiated, when switching is cheap, when they are well informed or able to integrate backward, or when end users are price-sensitive.
- Substitutes do the same job another way. Check their price, performance, switching cost and rate of improvement. They set a ceiling on price.

## Moat tests

Name the source of advantage and test it against the numbers.

- Production: process (indivisibility, complexity, rate of cost decline, legal protection, unique resources) and scale (distribution, purchasing, R&D, advertising). Signature: lower gross margin, high asset turnover. Scale is mostly local or regional.
- Consumer: habit, experience goods, switching costs and lock-in, network effects. Signature: high gross margin. Switching costs are financial, procedural (training, data migration, integration) and relational; gather retention, churn and contract length.
- External: subsidies, tariffs, quotas, licences, regulation.
- Efficient scale: a market that supports only a few profitable firms; the advantage belongs to whoever holds the position.
- Network effects count only where users interact (payments, marketplaces); hub-and-spoke networks are weak. Consumer-product leaders rarely exceed 40 percent share; with strong network effects leaders generally exceed 50 percent.
- A brand counts only if it raises willingness to pay or lowers supplier cost. Two of the ten owners of the world's top ten brands missed their cost of capital.

Never accept a moat from brand, size or share alone; require the spread against peers over a cycle, the signature, barrier evidence and a stated duration. Of 699 companies named in popular business success studies, 12 percent were superior beyond luck.

## Disruption signs

- Interaction: rivals in a repeated game with clear signals are stable; a single-play race for capacity is not.
- Innovation: sustaining, low-end disruptive or new-market disruptive. Ask whether products overshoot customer needs, competition is shifting to speed and convenience, and components are modularising.
- Erosion: costs no longer passed through, share lost to entrants in core segments, rising customer concentration, ROIC falling while revenue grows, rivals copying faster, staff leaving for competitors, suppliers or distributors integrating.
- Analogies: for a "this is the next X" claim, name the condition that made the first case work and test whether it holds here. Take base rates from the relevant regime, not one long average.

## Competitive advantage period

Duration is a judgment bounded by the sector's four-year return persistence (roic.md) and the listed-company half-life of about 10 years. Excess returns fade faster at high returns and heavy reinvestment, and the average period has been shrinking. Staples and health care fade slowly; technology and energy fast. A longer period needs evidence from the entry, moat and disruption tests. There is no period by industry to look up: state the years assumed and the evidence.

The market-implied period has averaged about 8 years, about 5 in highly competitive industries and about 15 in stable ones (US companies, 1976 to 2007). It shows what the price assumes, never a bound on the fundamental estimate (expectations.md, implied competitive advantage period).

## Limits

- Listed peer sets are older, larger and survivor-biased, and miss private rivals. State how many firms and what share of the market any concentration or share figure covers.
- For banks and insurers, judge ROE against the cost of equity.
- For a cyclical, use three- to five-year averages, never one year.
- In an emerging industry, shares are unstable; lean on the barrier and moat tests.
- A narrow question, such as pricing power, gets that technique and its evidence, not the full set.
